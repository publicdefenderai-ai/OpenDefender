One piece of feedback I have recently received about the site is that it is unclear whether the site is intended to serve litigants and their families, OR public defenders + case support agents. 

There are a few reasons for this confusion:
I made assumptions about where the greatest gaps and needs are. I relied on conversations with public defenders and Emily Galvin Almanza’s book to conclude that significant gaps exist in non-legal early advocacy (eg life and family support resources). BUT I have not spoken with litigants directly, and I have minimal information from case support agents. 
I have not articulated clear KPIs and goals
The site used to be geared more towards AI for legal guidance, along with tools for public defenders and attorneys. So it had aspects that were geared more towards lawyers. We have pivoted from that to target litigants and their families more directly, but some old artifacts/language remain
We also have not made clear paths/resources for case support agents. We should have paths that assist and improve their workflows, that directly make their lives easier, that have resources that they can then provide to their clients to improve education and substantive outcomes. 
One specific thing that could help is to create clear referral pathways to third parties - my client has this need, I want to refer them to X, and we give them a service that helps them find community organizations that fit a given need. 

As I look at the site, the homepage has a lot of content, and is presented in a cluttered manner. We do have pathways, but it could be more clear what the different pathways are and who they are for. We should have a pathway or pathways specifically for case support staff to help them assist their clients with life support. We should make the site more focused and less chaotic. I am not sure exactly how - one thought is to have the visitor fill out a quick questionnaire when they arrive at the site - who are you and what do you need - but that also puts a burden on the site visitor, so I am open to other ideas. 

We should also think through agentic solutions for some of these, again avoiding providing legal advice but doing things like finding the right third party referral resource given a user’s situation. OR some agentic solution to help an overwhelmed early advocacy case support staff member lighten their load by automating certain things. 

Take on the role of a senior product designer and manager, and outline a plan to address these issues, with prioritized tasks/todos to improve this point.

Summary
This 2009 article by J. McGregor Smyth, Jr. of the Bronx Defenders argues that the collateral consequences of criminal proceedings — job loss, housing eviction, deportation, loss of student loans, family separation — are often far more devastating than the criminal charges themselves. These "invisible punishments" trap individuals and entire communities in a cycle of poverty and recidivism.
Key findings and arguments:
Nearly one in four U.S. adults had a criminal record as of 2006, and the impact falls disproportionately on communities of poverty and of color. The article stresses that these consequences aren't limited to serious felonies: two turnstile-jumping convictions can trigger deportation, and simple marijuana possession cuts off federal student loans. More than a third of people arrested in New York are never convicted of anything, yet still suffer devastating consequences from the arrest record alone.
Criminal records are easy to create and nearly impossible to destroy. Dozens of agencies maintain overlapping databases, and a pilot review by the Bronx Defenders found that 62% of official state rap sheets contained at least one significant error — missing dispositions, unsealed cases, or unrecorded warrant vacaturs — all of which lead to wrongful denials of housing, jobs, and benefits.
The article proposes a "consolidated advocacy strategy" that redefines reentry as beginning at the moment of arrest, not upon prison release. It calls for integrating civil legal services (housing, immigration, employment, benefits) with criminal defense so that defenders can identify collateral consequences early and negotiate plea outcomes that avoid triggering them. It outlines a 10-step methodology: identify the barrier provision, examine the criminal record for errors, challenge legal hierarchy conflicts, raise constitutional issues, invoke state anti-discrimination protections, argue disparate racial impact, demand disability accommodations, pursue certificates of rehabilitation, challenge evidentiary deficiencies, and develop a compelling rehabilitation narrative.

Potential Resources That Could Be Built
Here are tools and resources that could help close the gaps this article identifies:
1. Collateral Consequences Lookup Tool — An interactive database where a user enters a jurisdiction, charge, and immigration status, and receives a plain-language summary of every collateral consequence triggered (housing, employment, immigration, student loans, licensing, etc.). The article notes these consequences are scattered across federal, state, and local statutes — consolidating them in one searchable interface would be transformative. The Uniform Collateral Consequences of Conviction Act called for exactly this, and the NIJ was set to fund a comprehensive survey.
2. Rap Sheet Error Checker / Audit Guide — A guided workflow or app that walks defenders and legal aid staff through systematically reviewing an official criminal history record for the three most common error types (missing dispositions, improperly unsealed cases, unrecorded warrant vacaturs). It could include templates for correction requests to DCJS-equivalent agencies in each state.
3. Plea Consequence Simulator — A tool for defense attorneys where they input a proposed plea offer and client profile (immigration status, housing situation, employment, student loan status) and get an immediate readout of downstream collateral consequences. This directly addresses the article's point that defenders, prosecutors, and judges often don't believe how severe these hidden punishments are.
4. Civil-Criminal Collaboration Toolkit — A resource package for legal aid organizations and public defender offices looking to co-locate or partner on integrated services. It would include intake screening questionnaires that flag collateral consequence risks, referral protocols, MOUs, and training curricula — essentially operationalizing the Bronx Defenders model described in the article.
5. Rehabilitation Narrative Builder — A guided template that helps advocates and clients assemble the "compelling narrative" the article describes as essential for discretionary proceedings. It would walk through the standard factors (nature of offense, time elapsed, relevance to opportunity, evidence of rehabilitation, turning point) and generate a structured submission package with prompts for supporting documentation (treatment records, employment history, letters of support).
6. Know-Your-Rights Resource for People with Records — A plain-language, multilingual guide (web and print) explaining anti-discrimination protections that exist in a user's state and locality, FCRA rights when a background check is run, how to request and correct rap sheets, and how to apply for certificates of relief from disabilities or certificates of good conduct. The article notes these tools are "vastly underutilized" because few people know they exist.
7. Background Check Accuracy Monitor — A tool that helps individuals request their own records from official repositories and commercial background screening companies, compare them for discrepancies, and generate dispute letters under FCRA and state equivalents. The article documents that only 45% of FBI arrest records even have dispositions attached.
Each of these addresses a specific structural gap the article identifies: the invisibility of consequences, the inaccuracy of records, the siloing of criminal and civil practice, and the lack of accessible information for the people most affected.

The Effects of Holistic Defense 
Anderson, Buenaventura & Heaton, "The Effects of Holistic Defense on Criminal Justice Outcomes," Harvard Law Review Vol. 132 (2019) — available at harvardlawreview.org.
What the Research Tells Us About Where to Intervene
The Anderson et al. study produces a striking finding: holistic defense reduced custodial sentences by 16% and sentence length by 24% — nearly 1.1 million fewer days of incarceration over ten years — without increasing recidivism. The key mechanism wasn't better trial lawyering in the traditional sense. It was better information gathering and communication: social workers and civil advocates identifying mitigating circumstances, collateral consequences, and client needs, then packaging that information so judges and prosecutors felt comfortable with less punitive outcomes.
This is critical for your platform design because it means the biggest gains come not from legal arguments but from structured information assembly that happens early in the case. The Bronx Defenders' advantage was most pronounced in drug cases (63% sentence reduction) and larceny cases (72% sentence reduction) — exactly the high-volume, poverty-driven charges where contextual information about a person's life most changes how the court sees them.
The study also found that conventional regression analysis actually got the direction of the effect wrong, making holistic defense look harmful. This happened because holistic defenders ended up with harder-to-observe case characteristics. The implication for your platform: any self-assessment or triage tool needs to be designed carefully so it doesn't inadvertently steer people away from help they need.

Highest-Impact Services for an Early Advocacy Platform
Based on both articles, here's what I'd prioritize, roughly in order of impact-per-dollar and feasibility:
1. Collateral Consequences Screener (at or before arraignment)
This is the single highest-value feature. The Bronx Defenders uses a checklist at arraignment covering employment, housing, mental health, children, immigration, student loans, public benefits, and more. The Smyth article documents that these consequences are scattered across federal, state, and local law and that even experienced attorneys don't know they exist. A guided questionnaire that takes a defendant (or their family member) through their situation and flags specific risks — "your guilty plea to this charge will make you ineligible for public housing for three years" or "two convictions of this type trigger deportation for lawful permanent residents" — would replicate the most distinctive feature of holistic defense. The key is jurisdiction-specific consequence mapping tied to actual charge codes.
2. Mitigating Narrative Builder
The Anderson study's most compelling explanation for why holistic defense works is the "information problem" theory: judges and prosecutors operate in assembly-line conditions, can't distinguish defendants who can be treated leniently from those who can't, and discount defense attorney claims because every attorney says their client is special. Social workers at the Bronx Defenders broke through this by assembling structured, credible mitigating packages. Your platform could walk defendants or their advocates through the standard factors from the Smyth article (nature/gravity of offense, time since prior convictions, relationship of offense to opportunity sought, evidence of rehabilitation, age at time of offense) and generate a structured document. Even for unrepresented defendants, having this ready at arraignment or a bail hearing changes the dynamic.
3. Rap Sheet Error Identification Guide
The Smyth article found 62% of official state rap sheets had at least one significant error, with only 45% of FBI arrest records even containing dispositions. These errors directly inflate bail amounts, influence plea offers, and trigger wrongful denials of housing and employment. A guided workflow that helps someone obtain their record and systematically check for the three most common error types (missing dispositions, improperly unsealed cases, unrecorded warrant vacaturs) would be immediately valuable. This is especially important because, as the Anderson study showed, predicted outcomes based on criminal history drive how the system treats a defendant from the first moment.
4. Pretrial Release Advocacy Toolkit
The Anderson study found holistic defense increased release on recognizance by 3.2% and reduced pretrial detention by 8.6%. This matters enormously because pretrial detention cascades into job loss, housing loss, and child custody disruption — the exact collateral consequences that drive recidivism. Your platform could help defendants or family members assemble the information bail judges consider (employment verification, community ties, housing stability, support network) into a structured presentation, and provide templates for requesting ROR or reduced bail.
5. Immediate Triage for Civil Legal Emergencies
Both articles emphasize that collateral consequences begin at arrest, not conviction. An arrest alone can trigger housing eviction proceedings, employment termination, child welfare investigations, and immigration detention. The platform should identify which of these risks are active for a specific user and provide immediate, actionable guidance: who to call to preserve a housing tenancy, what to tell an employer, how to document that children have alternative caregivers, and deadlines for responding to administrative actions. The Smyth article notes that families caught in the disruption of a pending criminal case often let landlord notices and welfare agency letters go unanswered until problems become emergencies.
6. Treatment and Service Connection Engine
The Anderson study found the largest sentence reductions in drug and larceny cases — precisely where connecting a defendant to treatment or stabilization services before sentencing shifts judicial perception most. A localized directory of drug treatment programs, anger management, mental health services, job training, and housing assistance, with information about intake requirements and wait times, would let defendants begin demonstrating rehabilitation effort from day one. The Bronx Defenders' ability to tell a judge "our client has already enrolled in a 12-week program" was a key driver of non-incarceration dispositions.
7. Know-Your-Rights Quick Reference
This is foundational. Covering FCRA rights when background checks are run, state anti-discrimination protections for people with records, sealing and expungement eligibility, and the existence of certificates of relief from disabilities or good conduct (which the Smyth article notes are "vastly underutilized" — fewer than 3,200 CRDs per year in New York despite 44,000+ disorderly conduct pleas in NYC alone). This should be available in multiple languages and at a low literacy level.

Design Principles the Research Supports
A few cross-cutting points from both studies that should shape how you build this:
Timing is everything. The whole thrust of both articles is that intervention at or before arraignment produces outsized returns. The platform should be designed for the 24-hour window between arrest and arraignment, and for the first week after arraignment when civil consequences begin triggering. Anything that requires a login process or extensive onboarding will miss the window.
Family members are the primary users. A person sitting in central booking can't use an app. Their partner, parent, or friend can. Design for the person on the outside who is trying to help and doesn't know where to start.
Jurisdiction specificity is non-negotiable. The Smyth article makes clear that collateral consequences vary wildly across federal, state, and local levels. A generic tool is minimally useful. Even a platform that covers just one or two high-volume jurisdictions deeply would be more valuable than a shallow national tool.
Credibility packaging matters more than legal arguments. The Anderson study's central insight is that the holistic model worked by making defense presentations credible to judges and prosecutors who are otherwise skeptical. Any documents the platform helps generate should look professional, cite specific statutes, and include verifiable claims rather than emotional appeals.
Drug and poverty-driven offenses are the sweet spot. That's where the evidence shows the largest effects, the highest volume, and the most receptive judicial audiences. If you need to scope an MVP, start there.
